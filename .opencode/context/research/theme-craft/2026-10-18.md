# Theme Craft — Controlling Idea, Lie/Truth, Motifs, Theme Audit

Ingested: 2026-10-18. Source: local `./theme-craft/` (SKILL.md + 5 references), authored for a Story Skills ecosystem. Adapted into Buchbinder as `skills/fiction/writing-techniques/theme-craft/` and wired into `/buchbinder manifest`, `/buchbinder ideation`, `/buchbinder specify`, `character-depth`, `/buchbinder revise`, and `/buchbinder review`.

## What it is

Plan and audit theme as a **working mechanism, not decoration**. Four pieces: the controlling idea (value + cause, in McKee's sense), the lie/truth machinery with three canonical arc types, antagonist design as the embodied counter-argument, motif tracking, and a revision-time theme audit.

## The controlling idea

Theme *words* ("justice", "redemption") can't guide a scene decision. A **controlling idea** can:

> **{Value} {triumphs | fails} because {cause}.**

- **Value** — the core moral value as a continuum (justice/injustice, love/hate, courage/cowardice).
- **Triumphs or fails** — the ending's verdict.
- **Cause** — the protagonist's action or quality that *produces* the verdict. This is what makes it usable: every scene is checked against whether the protagonist moves toward or away from the cause.

Record the **counter-premise** alongside: the antagonist's or world's opposing claim in the same form. The climax stages the two against each other and delivers a verdict.

**Working-premise workflow** (King vs. McKee/Truby split resolved as *working premise, revisable*): plan a working premise at start; use it during drafting ("which choice tests the value?"); audit in revision — if the draft argues something truer, rewrite the premise to match the draft.

**Premise quality check:** one sentence with a named value and a named cause; the cause is something the protagonist *does*; the counter-premise is a genuine belief someone could hold; the climax can stage both and pick a winner.

## Lie, truth, ghost wound, arc types

- **The lie** — the character's false belief ("Trusting people gets you killed"). It generates the **flaw** (behavior), **fear** (what the lie says will happen if they change), and **want** (the wrong external goal).
- **The truth** — the belief that replaces the lie; produces the **need**.
- **The ghost wound** — the specific backstory event that planted the lie. One scene, not a vague childhood: who, what happened, what they concluded.

**Three arc types** — record which one each significant character follows, because it dictates what the turning points must contain:

| Arc type | Shape | Turning points |
|----------|-------|----------------|
| `change-positive` | Lie → truth | The lie costs them; the crisis where it fails; the climax acting on the truth (default) |
| `change-negative` | Clings to the lie; it destroys them | Opportunities to choose truth, each refused; the lie's victories darkening; the climax doubling down. The truth must stay visible |
| `flat` | Already holds the truth; the *world* changes | **Tests of steadfastness**, not growth beats; each must cost more than the last |

Fields: `arc-type`, `lie`, `truth`, `ghost-wound`. Protagonist gets all four; supporting characters when their arc touches the theme.

## Antagonist design

The antagonist is the story's **embodied counter-argument**. The **worthy opponent** must be stronger than the protagonist in at least one dimension, or the victory feels unearned. The antagonist **believes they are right** — no one is the villain of their own story; if you can't write a paragraph defending their position, they are a plot device. **Plan the antagonist as a protagonist** (want, need, ghost wound, a plan that adapts, turning points). **Personify institutional opposition** with one true believer who embodies the system, keeping the institutional pressure separate from the character.

**Checklist:** has an edge; believes the counter-premise (defensible in a paragraph); has want/wound/plan; wins scenes; defeat at the climax is a *thematic* defeat, not just a physical one.

## Motif and symbolism

A motif is a recurring **concrete** element (object, image, phrase, action) that accrues meaning. **Plant** it literally and unremarkably, **vary** it in changed contexts earned by the scene, **vary with the character's arc**, and give it an **ending resonance** (the payoff). Two or three motifs per novel is plenty. A motif the narrative *explains* dies — never have a character say what it means. Track in a motif ledger (`planned` / `planted` / `paid-off`).

## Theme audit (revision)

Six checks: recover the working premise (or draft the one the manuscript actually argues); trace the value through each act (did the choice *cost* the protagonist?); check the ending against the opening's value-question; check the antagonist got a fair hearing; check every motif is paid off; check each arc's turning points match its arc type.

**The five audit questions:** Does the ending engage the opening's value-question? Is theme dramatized through consequence, not commentary? Is the motif paid off? Does the climax stage premise vs. counter-premise? Would the story collapse without the theme?

**Verdict:** `theme-holds` (minor fixes) or `theme-broken` (structural revision, with the specific scenes to rework).
