# Premise Workshop — Spark-to-Premise Method

Ingested: 2026-10-18. Source: local `./premise-workshop/` (SKILL.md + 4 references), authored for a Story Skills ecosystem. Adapted into Buchbinder as `skills/fiction/writing-techniques/premise-workshop/` and wired into `/buchbinder ideation` and `/buchbinder specify`.

## What it is

A pre-project method that turns a **spark** (an image, a what-if, a character, a setting, or a feeling) into a premise that can carry a book — *before* the project is built. The author owns the idea: offer options and tests, never decide for them.

## Pipeline

1. **Capture the spark** — take it in the user's words, do not improve it yet. Sort it (image / what-if / character / setting / feeling). Ask what drew them to it; that is often the book's real subject and must survive every later change.
2. **Generate 8–12 what-ifs** across angles — invert, raise the cost, give it to the wrong person, put a clock on it, move it, make it personal, find the first, find the last, ask who profits, collide genres. Present as a numbered list; the user picks 1–3 or combines. Filter out what-ifs with no person, no dramatizable scene, or that depend entirely on a twist.
3. **Workshop the logline** — protagonist + want + obstacle + stakes. Run every stress test; report pass / weak / fail with one sentence of why, and offer one revision per weak or fail.
4. **Draft premise and counter-premise** — the controlling idea (value + cause), not the logline. The counter-premise is the antagonist's or world's opposing argument in the same form. Test: could a reasonable person believe the counter-premise? If not, the antagonist is a straw man.
5. **Name the stakes on three levels** — external (what is lost in the world), relational (who is lost or betrayed), internal (what the protagonist becomes). At least two must be concrete and personal. Stakes must be able to worsen by the midpoint, or the premise stalls.
6. **Choose the form** — match the idea's moving parts (POV characters, threads, locations, time span, named cast, changes dramatized) to a form: novel, novella, novelette, short-story, flash, serial, picture-book, chapter-book.
7. **Brainstorm titles** across families (image, phrase from the world, character, contradiction, question/promise, genre signal); cut to five; test each for promise, shelf, sayability, searchability, and no spoiler. Check names against the project's entities.
8. **Sanity-check against comparable books** — two or three recent books the idea sits beside. Same shelf? What is the same, what is different? Never invent a title, author, year, prize, or sales figure; unverified comps are marked unverified.
9. **Hand off** — a one-screen brief (title, logline, premise, counter-premise, stakes, form, genre, POV/tense, themes, comps, language).

## Logline stress tests

| Test | Question | Common fix |
|------|----------|-----------|
| Active protagonist | Do they pursue something, or does the story happen to them? | Give them a goal they choose, even a wrong one |
| Concrete want | Could you film the moment they get or lose it? | Replace an abstract want with its visible form |
| Opposition that can win | Is the obstacle strong enough that failure is plausible? | Make the antagonist want the same thing |
| Built-in conflict | Does the premise force a collision, or only allow one? | Getting the want must cost something they value |
| A choice at the end | Can you imagine the final choice? | Name two things they value; design the ending to make them pick |
| Irony or hook | Something surprising — wrong person, unexpected cost, contradiction? | Apply "give it to the wrong person" |
| Sustains the length | Enough turns for the chosen form? | Shrink the form or add a complicating thread |
| Distinct | Could this describe a well-known book with names changed? | Change the protagonist, cost, or setting |

## Common premise failures

- **Situation, not story** — a world/condition with nobody pursuing anything. Fix: add a person whose goal the condition blocks.
- **Twist, not story** — everything depends on a reveal. Fix: ask what the protagonist wants before the reveal.
- **Passive sufferer** — things happen to the protagonist. Fix: find the point where they choose, and move it earlier.
- **Two books** — two strong premises fighting for the lead. Fix: choose one as the spine.
- **Small stakes told large** — a cosmic threat nobody on the page feels. Fix: shrink the scale until one relationship carries it.
